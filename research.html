Working papers

  • The Good Council: Deliberating Inequality in a Field Experiment
    with Severin Rapp, Lukas Lehner, Martin Haselmayer and Franziska Disslbacher. Stone Center on Socio-Economic Inequality Working Paper Series, No. 112.

    Can democratic innovations make political decision-making more informed and inclusive, or do they mostly engage those who are already active? This study contributes to debates on deliberative and participatory democracy with a pre-registered field experiment embedded in a citizens' assembly on wealth inequality in Austria. It examines how participation shapes redistributive preferences and democratic attitudes. By comparing participants, non-selected registrants, and a representative population sample, our design estimates the effects of assembly participation while accounting for self-selection. Participants gain knowledge and converge on a preferred tax design, yet we find no increases in political efficacy, engagement, or support for wealth taxation. These findings reflect strong self-selection among registrants, despite extensive recruitment efforts and attitudinal balancing on a broad issue-relevant item. This highlights both the promise and limits of democratic innovations: assemblies can foster informed agreement, yet their broader impact depends on inclusive framing, recruitment, and institutional anchoring.

    Media coverage: New York Times, Washington Post, BBC, Financial Times.

  • How Economic Elites Shape Public Tax Debates
    Single-authored

    Economic elites are widely seen as influential in tax policymaking, yet their role in shaping public tax debates remains less understood. This paper argues that economic elites occupy a central role in public debates on taxing the rich because journalists rely on them as authoritative interpreters of complex policies, while elites themselves can credibly speak to the feasibility and economic consequences of tax policies. To examine this, I develop a novel claim-level approach to studying public debates on taxing the rich, drawing on a newly collected dataset of newspaper coverage in Austria and the United Kingdom from the 1990s to 2024. The findings show that economic elites are highly prominent participants in debates on taxing the rich, appearing in around 26% of relevant articles in Austria and 51% in the UK. In line with their theorized role as policy experts, and in contrast to political elites, they justify their positions primarily through technical and structural frames, while moral justifications remain comparatively rare. This highlights that public debates over redistribution are not only a product of overt partisan contestation, but are also shaped by economic elites' ability to influence the informational environment in which citizens interpret and evaluate redistributive policies.

  • Business Ties in Cabinet: Ministers' Occupational Backgrounds and Economic Policymaking
    with Manuel Wagner and Laurenz Ennser-Jedenastik

    How close are ties between business and politics, and how does this translate into economic reform? Building on research showing that politicians' economic backgrounds influence political attitudes and behavior, we expect ministers with business ties to be more inclined to advance policies favorable to the business sector, while party insiderness limits this effect. To test this, we draw on two primary data sources covering seven Western European countries over three decades: detailed biographical data on ministers' occupational and partisan careers, and comprehensive information on economic reforms under their responsibility. By tracing the timing and duration of ministers' business and party affiliations, we assess how these connections shape policymaking. We contribute to debates on representative politics by examining whether the weakening of party government increases the influence of ministers' personal backgrounds, potentially undermining equal responsiveness across occupational groups.
